Replacing Single-Pane Windows in Older Homes

Single-pane and old aluminum windows in a pre-2000 home: what they do to comfort in a wet climate, how to tell what you have, and what replacing them fixes.

If your home was built before 2000, there is a good chance at least some of its windows are single-pane glass, aluminum frames, or early double-pane units whose seals gave out years ago. Around Roseburg and Eugene, these are the windows we replace most.

They are not always a crisis. Plenty of them still open and close fine. But in a climate with months of cold rain and hot valley summers, they are usually the least comfortable part of the house. Here is how to tell what you have and what replacing it changes.

How to Tell What Windows You Have

  • Single-pane: Hold a lighter or a small flashlight near the glass and count the reflections. Each pane of glass makes two, so two reflections means single-pane and four means double-pane. Single-pane glass also feels noticeably colder to the touch on a winter morning.
  • Aluminum frames: Thin metal frames, often silver or bronze, that feel cold in winter and hot in summer. In older homes these are often sliders.
  • Early double-pane: Four reflections, but the glass may look hazy between the panes, or the frames may be aluminum. A fogged unit has lost its seal and its insulating gas.
  • Original wood sashes: Often single-pane, sometimes with storm windows added. Check the sills and the bottom of the sash for soft or dark wood.

What Old Windows Do in a Western Oregon House

They sweat. A single-pane or aluminum window is the coldest surface in the room. In a house closed up for the wet season, indoor moisture condenses on it and runs down to the sill. Over the years that water stains trim, feeds mildew, and rots the wood below the glass.

They let the heat out, and in. The U.S. Department of Energy attributes 25 to 30% of a home's heating and cooling energy use to heat moving through windows, and old single-pane glass is the worst of it. In winter you feel a cold draft near the glass even when nothing is technically leaking. In summer, west-facing single-pane glass lets the afternoon heat straight into the room.

They let water into the wall. Older windows were often installed with little or no flashing. Decades of wind-driven rain can find its way around the frame and into the wall, which you may not see until the window comes out.

They let smoke and noise in. Loose sashes and worn weatherstripping let in more road noise and more wildfire smoke when it settles into the valley.

They are hard to live with. Painted-shut sashes, broken balances, and sliders that will not slide. Every bedroom needs a window that opens.

What Replacing Them Changes

A modern replacement window with low-E insulated glass changes how a room feels more than anything else on this list. The glass on the room side stays much closer to room temperature, so the cold draft and most of the condensation go away. Summer heat through west windows drops. The room gets quieter.

The 2023 Oregon Residential Specialty Code calls for windows rated U-0.27 or lower. For comparison, old single-pane glass is far above that. Ask for the U-factor in writing on any quote.

Replacement is also the chance to fix what the old window was hiding. A full-frame replacement lets us take the old window out, look at the framing, repair soft wood, and flash the opening properly before the new window goes in. That is the part that keeps water out for the next twenty years.

What We Usually Recommend

For most older homes around Roseburg and Eugene, we recommend the Andersen 100 Series. Its Fibrex composite frame does not rot or need paint, it is rated twice as strong as vinyl, and it comes in styles that suit older houses, including double-hung windows that look right in a traditional home. When you want real wood inside to match existing trim, we step up to the 400 Series. When quality vinyl fits better, we will say so.

Can You Replace Only Some of Them?

Yes. Many homeowners start with the windows that bother them most: the cold bedroom, the sweating kitchen window, the west-facing living room. We will tell you which windows are failing and which can wait. Doing the worst side of the house first is a reasonable plan.

Rebates for Single-Pane Windows

In Eugene, EWEB customers with electric heat who are replacing single-pane or metal-frame windows may qualify for a rebate. Our EWEB window rebate guide explains who qualifies. Energy Trust of Oregon ended its Oregon window incentives at the start of 2026, and the federal 25C window tax credit expired at the end of 2025, so outside EWEB territory there is usually no rebate right now.

The Short Version

If your home has single-pane glass, aluminum frames, or fogged early double-pane windows, they are probably making the house less comfortable every winter and every summer, and they may be letting water into the wall. Replacing them with a well-installed, low-E window fixes the cold draft, most of the condensation, and a lot of the summer heat.
