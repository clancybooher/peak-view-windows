Foggy Windows in Bend, Oregon: What They Mean

Cloudy glass in Bend, Oregon can be a free humidity fix or a failed seal. Learn the 60-second test before you spend a dime on foggy windows.

A foggy window means one of three things, and only one of them costs you money. Condensation on the inside of the glass is an indoor humidity problem, condensation on the outside on a cold morning usually means the window is doing its job, and a permanent haze between the two panes means the seal on that insulated glass unit has failed.

That third case is the only one you cannot clean away. If you are looking at foggy windows in Bend, Oregon and trying to work out which one you have, here is how to tell them apart in about sixty seconds, and what each actually costs to fix.

Condensation on the Inside of the Glass: Almost Always a Humidity Problem

If the fog is on the room side of the glass and wipes away with a finger, your window is not the problem. As Andersen Windows explains in its own condensation guide, condensation forms inside a window because indoor humidity is too high for the glass temperature, not because something is wrong with the window itself.

Central Oregon homes generate more moisture in winter than people expect: showers, cooking, houseplants, a house full of people breathing overnight. A tightly built or newly sealed home holds onto that moisture instead of letting it leak out the way a drafty, well-loved home used to. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency recommends keeping indoor relative humidity in roughly the 30 to 40 percent range in winter to help avoid this kind of condensation.

The fix is usually free: run the bathroom fan longer after a shower, vent the kitchen hood and dryer outside instead of into the house, crack a window on a mild day, or add a dehumidifier to a damp room. Left alone for years, ProVia notes that moisture can work into the wood trim around the window and lead to mold and rot. that's the real reason to take it seriously, not the fog itself.

Condensation on the Outside of the Glass: Usually a Sign the Window Is Working

If the fog or dew is on the outside of the glass and only shows up on cold, clear mornings, that's not a defect. It's often a sign your low-E glass is doing exactly what it's supposed to do.

Modern low-E coatings hold indoor heat inside so effectively that the outer pane runs colder than it would on an older, leakier window. On a still, clear morning that cold surface can drop below the outdoor dew point, and moisture in the air condenses on it the same way it beads up on a cold glass of water. Andersen Windows puts it plainly: condensation on the outside of a window is a sign of energy efficiency, because the glass is well insulated from the heat inside. ProVia makes the same point. exterior condensation means the window is functioning correctly, not failing. It needs no fix and usually burns off within an hour or two once the sun hits the glass. Homeowners who just upgraded their glass sometimes see this for the first time and assume something's wrong. It isn't. that's the upgrade working.

Moisture Between the Panes: The One That Doesn't Wipe Away

This is the case that actually costs money. If the haze sits between the two panes. Sometimes with a faint rainbow sheen, streaky film, or a chalky white deposit along the edge. No amount of cleaning touches it, because you can't reach it.

Insulated glass units are a sealed sandwich: two panes of glass, an air gap filled with insulating gas (usually argon), and a perimeter seal holding it together. When that edge seal breaks down, the gas leaks out and outside air, carrying moisture, leaks in. That moisture condenses on the inward-facing glass surfaces, sealed away from any rag or squeegee. Andersen Windows is direct about it: once the seal breaks and condensation starts forming between the panes, "you won't be able to prevent it from forming," and replacement is the real solution.

It's not just cosmetic, either. The U.S. Department of Energy's National Renewable Energy Laboratory, which studies insulating glass degradation, has found that a failed seal can push a unit's U-factor up by as much as 32 percent. in bad cases, close to single-pane performance.

You'll find "defogging" services that drill a small hole in the glass, flush out the moisture, and reseal it. Per Angi's reporting on the practice, it's a cosmetic fix, not a real one. It doesn't restore the lost gas fill or repair the broken seal, and the fog commonly returns within a year or two. Treat it as a way to buy a little time, not a permanent fix.

Why Seals Fail Faster Here Than in Most Places

We've written before about how Bend's high-desert climate puts windows through nearly a hundred-degree annual swing, from around -7°F on the coldest winter nights to 91°F on summer afternoons. That swing hits the seal every single day, not just once a year, as the gas trapped inside the unit heats and expands in the sun, then cools and contracts overnight. Thompson Creek, a national window manufacturer, notes that south- and west-facing windows commonly fail five to ten years earlier than north-facing windows on the same house, because they see bigger daily swings and more direct sun.

Elevation adds to it. Bend sits near 3,600 feet, and UV runs stronger up here. The EPA's UV Index guidance lists elevation as a factor that increases exposure, since there's less atmosphere overhead to filter it. That UV works directly on the rubber and polymer edge seal holding each unit together, drying it out and making it brittle years before a similar window would fail at sea level. Our low ambient humidity doesn't help either.

Late summer is a reasonable time to go look. By August, every seal in your house has been through a full season of hot, UV-heavy days and cool nights, on top of whatever the winter before it put them through. A seal already marginal from age or a bad batch tends to show it by now, and catching it while lead times are short beats discovering the same fogged unit in a November cold snap.

A 60-Second Checklist for Foggy Windows in Bend, Oregon

  1. Look at it in daylight. Does the haze sit on the surface, like it might wipe off, or does it look trapped inside the glass, maybe with a faint rainbow sheen or a chalky white line at the edge?
  1. Wipe the inside pane. If the moisture comes off with a dry cloth and the glass underneath is perfectly clear, that's surface condensation, not a seal failure.
  1. Note which side and what time of day. Outside fog that only appears on cold, clear mornings and burns off by mid-morning is dew. Inside fog after cooking or showering, or once the house is buttoned up for winter, is humidity.
  1. Check if it ever fully clears. Surface condensation, inside or outside, comes and goes with the weather. A failed seal doesn't. The haze is there in July the same as January.
  1. Walk the rest of the house. One hazy window is a single failed unit. Several fogging the same way at once often points to a batch of units reaching the end of their service life together, not one bad piece of glass.

Repair or Replace: The Honest Version

If what you have is inside or outside surface condensation, you don't need a window company. You need better ventilation or a little patience with the morning sun. We'll tell you that for free if you send us a photo.

If it's a failed seal, the right fix depends on what else is going on with the window:

  • Frame and hardware still solid, one unit gone bad: a glass-only IGU swap. pulling the failed sealed unit and setting a new one into the existing sash. is usually the cheapest real fix, and often all that's needed.
  • Sash warped, balances failing, or an old nonstandard size: full sash or unit replacement makes more sense than fighting a frame that's already going.
  • Frame itself rotted, or several windows failing at once: full-frame replacement is worth it. That's where we typically point Central Oregon homes toward the Andersen 100 Series, which meets the 2023 Oregon code's U-0.27 requirement that a re-glazed old sash generally won't.

A single fogged unit in an otherwise sound ten-year-old window is not a reason to replace the whole house. We'll say so plainly if that's genuinely all your home needs.

The Short Version

Fog on the inside means check your humidity. Fog on the outside on a cold morning means your windows are working. Fog that lives permanently between the panes means a seal has failed, and it won't clean up, wipe off, or fix itself. From there it's a question of how much of the window is actually compromised. Sometimes that's one pane of glass, sometimes it's the whole opening.
