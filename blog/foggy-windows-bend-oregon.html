Foggy Windows in Roseburg & Eugene, Explained

Cloudy glass can be a free humidity fix or a failed seal. How to tell which one you have in 60 seconds, written for wet Western Oregon winters.

A foggy window means one of three things, and only one of them means buying anything. Condensation on the inside of the glass is an indoor humidity problem. Condensation on the outside on a cool morning usually means the window is doing its job. A permanent haze between the two panes means the seal on that insulated glass unit has failed.

That third case is the only one you cannot clean away. Foggy windows are one of the most common calls we get from Roseburg and Eugene homeowners once the rain settles in each fall, so here is how to tell the three apart in about sixty seconds, and what fixes each one.

Condensation on the Inside of the Glass: Almost Always Humidity

If the fog is on the room side of the glass and wipes away with a finger, your window is not the problem. As Andersen Windows explains in its own condensation guide, condensation forms inside a window because indoor humidity is too high for the glass temperature, not because something is wrong with the window.

Western Oregon homes hold a lot of moisture in winter. The house is closed up for months, the air outside is damp, and showers, cooking, laundry, houseplants, and a family breathing overnight all add more. A tightly built or newly sealed home keeps that moisture in instead of leaking it out the way a drafty old house did. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency recommends keeping indoor relative humidity in roughly the 30 to 40 percent range in winter to help avoid this kind of condensation.

The fix is usually free: run the bathroom fan longer after a shower, vent the kitchen hood and dryer outside, and add a dehumidifier to a damp room or a daylight basement. Even on a rainy day, cold outside air holds less moisture than warm indoor air, so a little ventilation still helps. Left alone for years, ProVia notes that moisture can work into the wood trim around a window and lead to mold and rot. In a climate this wet, that is the real reason to take it seriously.

Condensation on the Outside of the Glass: Usually a Sign the Window Is Working

If the fog or dew is on the outside of the glass and only shows up on cool, clear, still mornings, that is not a defect. It is often a sign your low-E glass is doing exactly what it should.

Modern low-E coatings hold indoor heat in so well that the outer pane runs colder than it would on an older, leakier window. On a calm fall or spring morning in the valley, that cold surface can drop below the dew point, and moisture in the air condenses on it the same way it beads up on a cold glass of water. Andersen Windows puts it plainly: condensation on the outside of a window is a sign of energy efficiency. ProVia makes the same point. It needs no fix and usually burns off once the sun reaches the glass. Homeowners who recently upgraded sometimes see this for the first time and assume something is wrong. It is the upgrade working.

Moisture Between the Panes: The One That Does Not Wipe Away

This is the case that needs a real fix. If the haze sits between the two panes, sometimes with a faint rainbow sheen, a streaky film, or a chalky white line along the edge, no amount of cleaning touches it, because you cannot reach it.

Insulated glass units are a sealed sandwich: two panes of glass, a gap filled with insulating gas (usually argon), and a perimeter seal holding it together. When that edge seal breaks down, the gas leaks out and moist outside air leaks in. That moisture condenses on the inner glass surfaces, sealed away from any rag. Andersen Windows is direct about it: once the seal breaks and condensation forms between the panes, you will not be able to stop it, and replacement is the real solution.

It is not only cosmetic. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory, which studies insulating glass, has found that a failed seal can raise a unit's U-factor by as much as 32 percent. In bad cases the window performs close to single-pane glass.

You will find "defogging" services that drill a small hole in the glass, flush out the moisture, and reseal it. Per Angi's reporting on the practice, it is a cosmetic fix. It does not restore the lost gas or repair the broken seal, and the fog commonly returns within a year or two. Treat it as a way to buy time, not a permanent answer.

Why Seals Fail on Western Oregon Homes

An insulated glass seal flexes every day. The sun warms the gas inside the unit and it expands; the night cools it and it contracts. Thompson Creek, a national window manufacturer, notes that south- and west-facing windows commonly fail five to ten years earlier than north-facing windows on the same house, because they take the most direct sun and the biggest daily swings. In the valley, that is the glass taking the hot summer afternoons.

Water is the other half of the story here. Edge seals are built to stay dry. On an older window with failed glazing putty, a cracked bead, or clogged weep holes, rain that runs down the glass all winter can sit against the bottom edge of the unit for weeks. Months of standing water on a seal, year after year, wears it out faster than sun alone.

Fall is a good time to go look. Every seal in the house has been through a hot summer, and the first cold, wet weeks are when a failing unit starts to show it.

A 60-Second Checklist for Foggy Windows

  1. Look at it in daylight. Does the haze sit on the surface, like it might wipe off, or does it look trapped inside the glass, maybe with a rainbow sheen or a white line at the edge?
  2. Wipe the inside pane. If the moisture comes off with a dry cloth and the glass underneath is clear, that is surface condensation, not a seal failure.
  3. Note which side and what time of day. Outside fog that only appears on cool, clear mornings and burns off by mid-morning is dew. Inside fog after cooking or showering, or once the house is closed up for winter, is humidity.
  4. Check whether it ever fully clears. Surface condensation comes and goes with the weather. A failed seal does not. The haze is there in July the same as January.
  5. Check the sill while you are there. Press the wood below the glass. If it feels soft or looks dark and stained, water has been getting in, and that changes what the right fix is.
  6. Walk the rest of the house. One hazy window is a single failed unit. Several fogging the same way often means a batch of units reaching the end of their life together.

Repair or Replace: The Honest Version

If what you have is inside or outside surface condensation, you do not need a window company. You need better ventilation or a little patience with the morning sun. We will tell you that for free if you send us a photo.

If it is a failed seal, the right fix depends on the rest of the window:

  • Frame and hardware still solid, one unit gone bad: a glass-only swap, pulling the failed sealed unit and setting a new one into the existing sash, is usually the simplest real fix, and often all you need.
  • Sash warped, balances failing, or an odd older size: replacing the sash or the whole unit makes more sense than fighting a frame that is already going.
  • Rot in the frame or sill, or several windows failing at once: full-frame replacement is worth it. That lets us open the wall, fix the flashing, and seal the opening properly. It is where we usually point homeowners toward the Andersen 100 Series, which meets the 2023 Oregon code's U-0.27 requirement and will not rot the way an old wood sill does.

A single fogged unit in an otherwise sound ten-year-old window is not a reason to replace the whole house. We will say so plainly if that is all your home needs.

The Short Version

Fog on the inside means check your humidity. Fog on the outside on a cool morning means your windows are working. Fog that lives between the panes means a seal has failed, and it will not wipe off or fix itself. From there it is a question of how much of the window is compromised. Sometimes that is one pane of glass. Sometimes it is the whole opening.
