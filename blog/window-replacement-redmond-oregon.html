Window Replacement in Redmond, Oregon

Window replacement in Redmond, Oregon runs on different elevation, wind, and housing stock than Bend. Here is what actually applies to your home.

Window replacement in Redmond, Oregon follows the same Oregon building code as Bend, but three local details change how you plan the job: more open wind exposure, a housing stock concentrated in one growth era, and and a permit question with a simpler answer than most homeowners expect.

Redmond sits roughly 600 feet lower than Bend and takes more wind out on the flats. If you are comparing quotes, those three things — exposure, house vintage, and timing — drive the decision, along with getting the order placed before winter closes in.

Redmond sits at roughly 3,000 feet in elevation, close to 600 feet lower than Bend's roughly 3,600 feet, and both cities sit inside Deschutes County, which the state's climate zone map places entirely in IECC climate zone 5B. That means the same 2023 Oregon Residential Specialty Code requirement — a window U-factor of 0.27 or lower — applies to a house in Redmond exactly the way it applies to a house in Bend. What changes is everything around that code minimum.

What makes window replacement in Redmond, Oregon different from Bend's?

Lower elevation means slightly less extreme cold on paper, but it doesn't mean an easier climate to build in. Redmond sits on more open, flatter ground than many Bend neighborhoods tucked into pine cover, and that terrain takes more sustained wind. We don't have a clean city-to-city wind statistic to hand you, but it shows up in the work: crews replacing windows in Redmond consistently see more dust and grit finding its way in around a worn sash or gapped weatherstripping than they do on a comparable job in a more sheltered Bend yard. A window that's merely inefficient in a calm spot becomes a window that's actively letting outside air and dust into the house once it's sat in open wind for a couple of decades.

None of that changes what the code requires — U-0.27 is U-0.27 whether the address is in Redmond or Bend. It changes how much a failing window actually costs you in day-to-day comfort. If you want the fuller climate-and-energy picture we give Bend homeowners, here's what we tell them — most of it applies just as directly in Redmond.

Why are so many Redmond windows reaching the end of their life at once?

Redmond had one of the fastest growth stretches of any city in Oregon starting in the 1990s and running through the 2000s — U.S. Census figures show the city's population going from 13,481 in 2000 to 26,215 in 2010, essentially doubling in a decade. Homes built before 2000, and the wave that followed right after it, make up a large share of what's now standing in Redmond's established neighborhoods, and most of that housing stock went up with standard builder-grade double-pane insulated glass — good, code-compliant windows for their era, not custom units.

That glass has a working life. Industry estimates put typical insulated glass unit (IGU) seal life at roughly 10 to 20 years: budget seals from that era often start failing in the 10-to-15-year range, while better-built units with more durable spacer systems can run 25 to 30 years before the seal gives out. Once a seal fails, the inert gas fill between the panes is gone, moisture works its way in, and the glass fogs from the inside — a problem cleaning never fixes, because the fog is trapped between two sealed panes of glass.

That math puts a meaningful share of homes built before 2000 in Redmond's growth-era neighborhoods at or past the point where the original glass is doing its job. Here's what that tends to look like from house to house:

Build eraWhat crews commonly seeWhat it usually means
Before 1990Single-pane glass, or early dual-pane units with visibly fogged corners and sashes painted shutInsulating value is gone or going — full-unit replacement is the fix
1990s–2000s (Redmond’s biggest growth years)Hazy or milky glass at the edges, sluggish cranks or balances, a draft you can feel at the sashBuilder-grade seals are at or past typical service life
2010 and newerSeals still tight, hardware operates smoothlyUsually fine — worth a spot check, not a replacement

None of this means every window in a home from that era needs replacing today — some units, especially better-built ones from later in the 2000s, still have years left. It means it's worth an actual look rather than an assumption either way.

Why schedule window replacement before the first freeze, not after?

Because the order goes in long before the crew shows up. Windows are manufactured to order — frame size, glass package, and finish are specific to your openings — and that lead time is real, even though it varies by manufacturer and by how busy that manufacturer is that season. We give you the actual lead time in writing with your quote rather than a generic number, because quoting a figure we can't stand behind doesn't help you plan.

What we can tell you in general terms: an order placed in early fall is what typically lands and gets installed before Central Oregon's first hard freeze. Wait until the cold actually arrives and you're usually looking at a spring install instead, since sealants and foam need workable temperatures to cure properly, and a house with an open window opening in January is not where anyone wants to be mid-project. If you're thinking about window replacement for a Redmond home this year, late August is closer to the front of that runway than the back of it.

What should you ask for in a Redmond replacement window?

Start with the U-factor. Any quote for window replacement in Redmond should state a U-factor of 0.27 or lower in writing — that's the Oregon code minimum for this zone, not an upgrade. For frame material, we typically recommend the Andersen 100 Series: its Fibrex composite frame resists the kind of warping and fading that comes from years of high-desert sun and temperature swings, and the color runs through the material rather than sitting on top as paint. For a step up with a real wood interior, the 400 Series is the option.

Beyond the product itself, ask:

  • Who is measuring the opening and who is actually doing the work — not just who is selling it
  • Whether the installer is licensed, insured, and can give you a CCB number to verify on Oregon's own site
  • How the opening gets sealed and trimmed — professional-grade flashing and high-quality caulk and foam sealing around the frame matter more to long-term performance than the glass package does
  • What the manufacturing lead time is, in writing, before you commit to a schedule

A window that's rated well but installed poorly on an exposed Redmond lot will leak air at the frame regardless of what the glass is rated for. The install is most of the outcome.
